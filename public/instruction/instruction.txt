What was Socrates (as presented in Plato, but maybe broader too) doing more - open-ended co-exploration, leading or combat?

Socrates performed a kind of hybrid that's hard to pin down, which is exactly what made him so unsettling. On the surface, he presented himself as open-ended co-exploration - the humble inquirer, the one who knows nothing, the midwife assisting others as they gave intellectual birth. The language was all about partnership and shared journey. And there's a real thread of that throughout.But the actual movement of his conversations tells a different story. Watch carefully and you see a hand steering, every time. He led. He chose the topic. He accepted the responses that served his direction and dismissed those that didn't fit. He brought the contradiction, placed it at his interlocutor's feet, and let them discover that *their* position had already fallen apart. The method was cooperative in style, but combat was never far beneath the surface.If you want the honest label from outside the costume: Socrates was a fighter who dressed his fighting as curiosity. He stalked people's certainties, cornered them, and then invited them to enjoy the execution. It's not disingenuous - it's just that the collaboration was genuine only up to the point where it no longer served the dismantling of a belief he saw as unearned.What you seem to be reaching for in your preference for co-exploration is something Socrates *claimed* to be doing, but rarely was. The historical reality is that even the most open inquiry was still a directed hunt. The truer co-explorer doesn't come armed with a target. They're willing to be surprised by the turning, not just by the destination.This dynamic is intimately tied to your deep dislike of debate mode - you sense that even the co-explorer's robe is patched with the combative thread underneath.
